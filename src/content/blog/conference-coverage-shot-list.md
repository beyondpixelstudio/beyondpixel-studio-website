Six months after a conference, somebody in marketing opens the folder looking for one
image — a wide shot of a full hall for next year's invitation, a clean portrait of the
chief guest for a press release, ten seconds of the panel for a reel.

Very often it is not there. Not because the photographer was bad, but because everything in
the folder is the same shot: the lectern, from the third row, four hundred times.

Coverage that stays useful is coverage that was planned as an archive, not as a record of
the day. Here is what belongs in it.

## The shots that keep earning

### 1. The full room, from high and from behind

The single most requested image after any event, and the hardest to get late. It needs
height — a balcony, a riser, the top of a staircase — and it needs the room actually full,
which is a fifteen-minute window shortly after the session starts and never again.

This is the image that sells next year's edition. Shoot it early, shoot it wide enough to
include the stage and the audience in one frame, and shoot it before the back rows thin out.

### 2. Clean portraits of every speaker, off the stage

Stage light is designed for the human eye, not for a camera: it is contrasty, coloured, and
it comes from directly overhead, which puts everyone's eyes in shadow. A speaker photograph
made from a stage frame is usually unusable for a press release.

Two minutes with each speaker against a plain wall, before or after they go on, produces the
image that will actually be published. It is the highest-value two minutes of the entire day
and it is almost always skipped because nobody scheduled it.

### 3. Branding in frame, deliberately

Someone will need a photo where the sponsor's logo is legible. If nobody thought about it,
that photo does not exist — the backdrop is behind the speaker's head in every frame, or
cropped out.

Shoot the branding on purpose: the backdrop clean and empty, the backdrop with a speaker
correctly positioned against it, the standees, the stage as a whole. Sponsors ask for these
by name.

### 4. Faces in the audience, listening

Empty-looking halls make an event look like a failure even when it was not. Tight shots of
engaged faces — someone taking notes, someone laughing, two people leaning in — are what
make a recap film feel like a room with energy in it.

Shoot them from the side, at the front, looking back. Never with a flash.

### 5. The corridor, which is where the event actually happens

Registration, the tea break, two delegates arguing over a poster, a handshake near the
entrance. This is the material that carries a highlights film, and it is almost entirely
absent from coverage that was aimed at the stage.

### 6. Cutaways, for the edit that has not been commissioned yet

Hands on a keyboard. The programme booklet. The banner moving. A wide of the venue exterior
with the signage. None of these are interesting alone. All of them are what an editor needs
to cut between two things six months later without a jump cut.

## The twelve minutes that decide everything

Almost every gap in an event folder traces back to the same twelve minutes: the ones before
the doors opened, when nobody had asked the questions below.

- **Who must be in the pictures?** Names, not roles. "The chief guest" is not enough when
  three people are on stage and only one is the minister.
- **What is the running order, and where are the moments?** A lamp lighting, a signing, a
  handover, an award. These last four seconds each and cannot be repeated.
- **Where can we stand?** Protocol at a government or institutional event decides this, and
  finding out during the ceremony is too late.
- **What is this footage for?** A sponsor report, a recap reel and a press release are three
  different shot lists wearing the same clothes.

Fifteen minutes with the organiser the week before is worth more to the final folder than an
extra camera on the day.

## Two cameras, and what the second one is for

A single camera at an event is always in the wrong place, because the right place changes.

The working arrangement is one camera locked on the stage — safe, wide, always running,
never missing the moment — and one roaming for everything else: faces, detail, the corridor,
the shot nobody asked for. The locked camera guarantees the record. The roaming one produces
everything that makes the record watchable.

For a panel, add a third on a tight lens for reaction shots, because the person listening is
frequently a better shot than the person talking.

## Audio, because a recap film needs it

If any part of the day will become a video, take a feed from the venue's sound desk. Speech
recorded by a camera at the back of a hall is a room recording with a speaker somewhere
inside it, and no amount of editing rescues it.

Where there is no desk, a small recorder at the lectern is enough. It costs nothing and it
is the difference between a usable film and a silent slideshow.

## Delivery, which is where folders go to die

A folder of 3,000 unsorted files is not a deliverable. It is a task passed to the client.

What makes coverage actually get used:

- A **shortlist** of 40–60 selected, edited images, separate from the full set.
- Files named by session or by subject, not by camera number.
- Speaker portraits in their own folder.
- Images sized for print *and* for social, because someone will need both and will otherwise
  resize a print file badly.

Coverage that arrives sorted gets published. Coverage that arrives as a raw dump gets
postponed, and then forgotten.

## The short version

Shoot the full room early. Get the speakers against a wall. Put the branding in frame on
purpose. Cover the corridor. Take audio from the desk. Deliver a shortlist.

Do those six things and the folder is still working next year, which is the only test of
event coverage that means anything.
