Most people commissioning a corporate video have not commissioned one before. That is
normal — it is not an annual purchase, and the vocabulary is unfamiliar enough that it is
hard to tell a good answer from a confident one.

What follows is the list we would use if we were on the buying side of the table in
Bhubaneswar. Some of these questions we answer well. Some of them are uncomfortable for
us too. They are here because they are the ones that decide whether a project goes
smoothly, not because they flatter anybody.

## 1. Who is actually turning up on the day?

This is the single most useful question in the list, and it is the one most rarely asked.

In this market a great deal of production work is subcontracted. The person who takes your
brief, quotes the job and earns your confidence is often not the person who arrives with a
camera. Sometimes that is fine. Often it means the crew on your shoot has never spoken to
you, has not read the brief, and is being paid a day rate by somebody whose margin depends
on how little they spend on the day.

Ask directly: *are the people shooting this your own staff?* Then ask the follow-up that
matters — *if the person you have named is unavailable, what happens?* A studio with its own
crew will answer that plainly. A broker will answer it vaguely.

## 2. Whose equipment is it?

Same logic, different failure. Rented kit is not a problem in itself — everybody hires in
speciality equipment occasionally. The problem is a studio whose *entire* kit list is
rented, because rental availability is not guaranteed and substitutions happen the morning
of the shoot.

The practical consequence is not usually a disaster. It is a smaller drone than you were
shown, a second camera that does not match the first, or a lens that changes how the room
looks. You will not notice on the day. You will notice in the edit, when two angles of the
same interview do not cut together cleanly.

## 3. Can I see work in my sector, not just your showreel?

A showreel is assembled from the best three seconds of every job a studio has ever done. It
tells you what their ceiling looks like. It does not tell you what your project will look
like.

Ask for a full piece of work from a sector close to yours — a campus film, an industrial
site, a convocation, whatever is nearest. Watch it end to end. A studio that has done work
like yours will have something to show. A studio that has not should say so, and if the
answer is an unrelated example stretched to fit, that is your answer.

## 4. What exactly is in the quote?

A lump sum tells you nothing. It cannot be compared against another lump sum, and it cannot
be reduced, because you cannot see what is in it.

An itemised quote lists crew, kit, shoot days, travel, edit time, revisions and each
deliverable as separate lines. That does two things. It lets you compare two studios
honestly — often you will find the "expensive" one is quoting three cameras and two edit
days against the other's one and one. And it lets you take things out. If the drone shot is
not worth the money to you, you can see what removing it saves.

## 5. What is not in the quote?

The corollary, and the one that produces most disputes. The lines that go missing most often:

- **Travel and overnight stay** for shoots outside the city
- **Additional revisions** beyond an unstated included number
- **Raw footage** handover
- **Versions** — a 16:9 master is not the same as vertical cuts for social
- **Music licensing** for commercial use
- **Overtime** if the event runs long, which events do

None of these are unreasonable to charge for. All of them are unreasonable to discover
afterwards.

## 6. How many revisions are included, and what counts as one?

"Two rounds of revisions" means very little without a definition. One consolidated set of
notes from your team is a round. Three people sending contradictory notes on three
different days is three rounds, and that is how a project quietly doubles in cost.

Agree who gives feedback, that it arrives together, and what happens when it does not.

## 7. Who owns the finished video?

You should, outright, in writing, before the shoot.

This is standard and most studios will agree without argument. It is worth confirming
anyway, because the moment it matters is the moment it has become a problem — a
relationship has ended, or you want to re-cut the film two years later and cannot.

## 8. Do I get the raw footage?

Separate question, different answer. Many studios do not hand over raw footage by default,
and there are defensible reasons — it is large, it is unusable without editing skill, and it
can be re-cut badly under the studio's name.

But if you want it, say so at quote stage. Retrospective requests for raw footage from a
shoot six months ago tend to be met with a storage charge or an apology.

## 9. What happens if the weather, the venue or the schedule changes?

Every event shoot in Odisha between June and September carries a monsoon risk. Every
corporate shoot carries the risk that the person you are filming is called into a meeting.

The answer you want is not "that will not happen." It is a stated policy: what a
postponement costs, how much notice avoids a charge, and whether a rain day is built into
the quote.

## 10. What is the delivery timeline, and what does it depend on?

Two to four weeks is normal for a straightforward single-location corporate film. What
matters more than the number is what it hinges on — usually your approval of a script, and
your feedback on the first cut.

A studio that gives you a delivery date without telling you what they need from you and
when has not planned the project. Ask for the dates they need things *from you*.

## 11. Have you worked with government or institutional clients?

If you are a department, a PSU or a university, this is not a vanity question.

Institutional shoots carry requirements ordinary corporate work does not: access
clearances, protocol around dignitaries, fixed camera positions agreed with security, and a
crew that knows not to move during an address. A studio that has not done it will not know
what it does not know, and the failure mode is not a bad video — it is being asked to stop
filming.

## 12. Will you tell me when video is the wrong answer?

The most useful thing a production company ever told us about our own work was that a film
we wanted to make would not achieve what we wanted it to achieve.

A studio that says yes to everything is optimising for the invoice. Ask what they would
talk you out of. The answer tells you whether you are buying a supplier or a partner.

## A short note on price

We are deliberately not publishing a per-minute rate, and you should be sceptical of anyone
who does without qualification.

Published Indian market figures for corporate video run from roughly ₹25,000 to well over
₹1,00,000 per finished minute, and metro rates typically sit 20–40% above smaller cities.
That range is so wide it is close to useless as a planning number, because it is being
driven by variables that change completely between two projects that both produce "a
two-minute film": shoot days, crew size, whether there is a script, a presenter, actors,
voiceover, animation, and how many versions come out the other end.

The honest way to price a video is to describe the job and get it itemised. Then the number
means something, and you can change it.

## If you are comparing studios in Bhubaneswar

Put the same brief in front of each of them, in writing, with the same detail. Ask all of
them for an itemised quote. Then compare line by line rather than total by total.

You will usually find the totals are closer than they looked, and that the difference is
one studio quoting for something the other quietly left out — which is exactly the thing you
wanted to find out before signing, rather than afterwards.
