Most studio bookings that go wrong go wrong in the first hour. The floor is smaller than it
looked in the photographs, the light you assumed was included is extra, the power will not take
your kit, or the air conditioning is so loud the interview has to be re-recorded.

Every one of those can be found out with a message before you book. Here is what to ask.

## The space

**1. Floor size — and shooting distance.** The number that matters is not the room's area but how
far the camera can get from the subject while keeping the background in frame. A full-length
fashion shot on a longer lens needs far more depth than a tabletop product. Send your frame and
lens; ask whether it fits.

**2. Ceiling height.** It decides whether lights can go overhead, whether a tall subject can be
shot from a low angle without the ceiling in frame, and whether a boom microphone fits above
standing talent.

**3. The backdrop.** Is there a cyclorama or infinity cove, seamless paper, a chroma screen? What
colours, what widths? If the job needs a clean white floor in frame, a paper roll on a wall is not
the same as a cove. (Our guide to [cyclorama and chroma](/blog/cyclorama-vs-chroma-studio/)
covers which you need.)

## The kit

**4. What lighting and grip is included in the rate** — and what is itemised. Continuous LED or
strobe? Modifiers — softboxes, umbrellas, grids? C-stands, flags, reflectors, sandbags? "Lighting
available" can mean anything from a full kit to two lamps in a cupboard.

**5. Power.** How many circuits, and can they take your heaviest lights running together? Where
are the sockets? A studio that trips when two big lights come on costs you the morning.

**6. Tethering and client viewing.** Is there a monitor and a station where the client can watch
frames arrive, rather than crowding behind the photographer?

## The conditions

**7. Sound, if you are recording video.** Listen for air conditioning, fans, traffic, a lift
shaft, a neighbour's generator. None of these are obvious until you put on headphones, and all of
them ruin interviews.

**8. Light control.** Can daylight be blacked out? Mixed daylight and studio light changes
colour through the day and makes a consistent catalogue impossible.

**9. Changing, hair and makeup.** A changing area with a mirror, power for tools, and somewhere
talent can wait without walking through the set.

## The booking

**10. What counts as your hours.** Does setup and strike come out of your booking? Is there a
minimum booking — two hours, four, a full day? What does overtime cost, and is it charged by the
hour or the half-day?

**11. Load-in and parking.** Where can a vehicle stop to unload heavy cases? Is there a lift? A
difficult load-in can eat the first forty minutes of a short booking.

**12. Damage and wear.** Who pays if the cove needs repainting after a shoot with shoes, props or
paint on it? Is there a deposit? Better to know before than to argue after.

## Bonus: people

Is someone from the studio on site who knows where everything is? Can you add a photographer, a
camera operator or a gaffer to the booking if your own crew is short? A studio that can fill a
gap in your team on the day is worth more than one with a slightly larger floor.

## A message to send before you book

> Shooting [what] for [how long]. Frame: [full length / waist up / tabletop], [number] people in
> shot, [static / moving]. Need: [cove / paper / chroma], [lighting you need]. Bringing: [your
> kit]. Arriving by [vehicle] at [time]. Can the floor take this, and what is included in the rate?

Any studio worth booking will give you a straight yes or no.
