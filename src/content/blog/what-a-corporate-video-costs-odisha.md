The first question on almost every call is the same one: *what does a corporate video cost?*
It is a fair question and it deserves a better answer than "it depends" — but it also
deserves a better answer than a number invented before anyone has asked what the video is
for.

Here is the honest version. There is a real market range, it is wide, and where your job
falls inside it is decided by about six things. Once you know what those six are, you can
read any quote you are given — ours or anyone else's — and tell whether it is expensive,
cheap, or simply describing a different job to the one you had in mind.

## Why we do not publish a price list

Because a price list would be wrong on the day you needed it.

A two-minute company profile shot in one office in Bhubaneswar with a two-person crew, and
a two-minute company profile shot across three plants in two districts with a director, a
gaffer and a drone, are the same line on a price list and nowhere near the same job. Publish
one number and you either overcharge the first client or under-deliver to the second.

So every job here is quoted individually and itemised — crew, equipment, days and
deliverables appear as separate lines, and that written quote is the agreement. What follows
is not a price list. It is the reasoning behind one.

## The market range, stated plainly

For context rather than as our rates: published Indian industry guides in 2026 put
live-action corporate work broadly in the range of **₹50,000 to ₹2,00,000 for a finished
two-minute film**, with animation and 3D product work priced per minute and running higher
again. Those same guides note that metro rates in Mumbai, Delhi and Bengaluru typically run
20–40% above smaller cities, which is a large part of why production in Odisha is
competitive on cost without being competitive on corners.

That is a four-fold spread on the same nominal deliverable. Everything below explains the
spread.

## 1. Shoot days, which are the real unit

Video is not priced by the minute of output. It is priced by the day of input.

A finished two-minute film can come from half a day or from four days, and the finished
length tells you almost nothing about which. What decides it is how many locations, how many
people have to be filmed, and how much of the day is spent waiting — for a plant to shut
down, for a minister to arrive, for the light to come round the building.

If you want one number to sanity-check a quote against, it is the day count, not the runtime.

## 2. Crew size, which follows from the format

An interview-led film with a locked-off camera and a lapel mic needs two people. A scripted
brand film with lighting setups, a moving camera and sound recorded properly needs five or
six, because those jobs cannot be done by the same person at the same time.

Adding crew is not padding. It is the difference between a shot you light and a shot you
find. But it is also the single fastest way to move a budget, which is why the format
conversation has to happen before the money conversation, not after.

## 3. How much has to be built rather than found

Filming your factory as it runs is cheap. Filming your factory as you wish it looked is not.

Art direction, set dressing, hired props, a location that is not yours, actors rather than
employees, wardrobe — each of these is a real line item, and each is a decision made at the
script stage. Most budget shocks we see come from a script that quietly assumes a built
world and a budget that assumed a documentary one.

## 4. Post-production, where the invisible work lives

Cutting is the part clients picture. It is rarely the expensive part.

The expensive part is everything after the cut is locked: colour grading, sound mix,
motion graphics, titles in two languages, subtitles, a licensed music track rather than a
free one, and a voice-over recorded properly. A simple edit can be a day. A film with
graphics and a proper grade is a week, and the week is real work even though nobody was
holding a camera during it.

## 5. Deliverables, which is the multiplier nobody budgets for

This is the one that catches people.

One film is one film. One film plus a 60-second cut, a 30-second cut, a square version for
LinkedIn, a vertical version for Instagram, and a version with burned-in subtitles is
**six** deliverables — six timelines, six exports, six rounds of approval. The footage cost
the same. The finishing did not.

Decide the deliverable list before the shoot, not after. It changes what gets filmed, not
just what gets exported: a vertical cut needs framing that leaves room to crop, and you
cannot add that later.

## 6. The deadline

Industry guides put the premium for compressed timelines at 25–50%, and that matches what
we see. It is not opportunism — it is overtime, it is a second editor, it is pulling crew
off something else. A fortnight is normal. Forty-eight hours is a different product.

## What moves it less than people expect

- **Camera body.** Beyond a professional baseline, the difference between bodies shows up
  far less on the finished film than lighting and sound do.
- **Runtime, past a point.** Trimming three minutes to two saves an edit session, not a
  shoot day.
- **Resolution.** Shooting 4K instead of 1080p costs storage and grading time, not a
  different crew.

## How to compare three quotes properly

Ask each company for the same four things, and the comparison becomes possible:

1. **Shoot days**, and how many locations each covers.
2. **Crew list**, by role.
3. **Deliverables**, every cut and every version, listed separately.
4. **Revision rounds** included, and what a further round costs.

A quote that is a single lump sum with no day count is not cheaper or dearer than the
others. It is simply not comparable, and the gaps in it are where the disagreement will
happen in month two.
