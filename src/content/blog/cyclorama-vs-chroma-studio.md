Two studio setups get confused more than any others, because both involve a large plain
background. They do opposite jobs.

A **cyclorama** makes the background *disappear*. A **chroma key** backdrop lets you *replace*
it. Book the wrong one and you either spend a day in post-production cutting out a subject that
should have been shot clean, or you shoot on green and discover the scene you wanted to put
behind them did not need replacing at all.

## What a cyclorama does

A cyclorama — "cyc", or infinity cove — is a wall that curves smoothly into the floor, with no
visible corner. Lit evenly, the curve erases the horizon line, so the subject appears to float in
an endless space: pure white, grey, or whatever colour the cove is painted.

It is the setup for:

- **full-length fashion and portraits**, where the floor has to be in frame without a visible join;
- **product on white**, including large products that will not fit on a table sweep;
- **movement and dance**, where the subject needs room to travel without leaving the background;
- **clean interview and explainer video** with a minimal look.

Getting a true white from a cyc is a lighting job. The cove is lit separately from the subject —
usually brighter than the subject by about a stop — so it reads pure white without spilling light
back onto the person. It also needs care: shoe covers on the floor, and a repaint when the curve
gets scuffed.

## What chroma key does

Chroma key replaces a single, saturated colour — almost always green or blue — with other footage
or graphics in post-production. Everything that is that colour becomes transparent.

It is the setup for:

- **presenters over graphics**, news-style;
- **talent composited into a location** you cannot travel to, or one that does not exist;
- **product in a generated or 3D scene**;
- **virtual sets** for recurring content.

## Green or blue?

- **Green** is the default. Camera sensors are most sensitive to green, so a green screen needs
  less light to key cleanly, and it holds up better with the compressed footage many cameras
  record.
- **Blue** spills less and suits subjects wearing green, holding plants, or with fair hair that
  green tends to contaminate. It needs more light.

## The rules for a clean key

Most bad chroma keys are made on set, not in the edit:

1. **Distance.** Keep the subject well clear of the backdrop — a couple of metres or more — so the
   green does not bounce onto their shoulders and hair.
2. **Light the backdrop and the subject separately.** The screen wants flat, even light with no
   hot spots; the subject wants their own key light. One set of lights doing both jobs does
   neither.
3. **No green on the subject.** Clothing, jewellery that reflects, even a green logo on a mug.
   Anything green disappears.
4. **Avoid wrinkles and seams** in the backdrop; they key as uneven patches.
5. **Freeze the motion.** A faster shutter reduces motion blur on hands and hair, which is where
   keys fall apart.
6. **Record the best quality the camera allows.** Higher bit depth and less compression give the
   keyer more colour information at the edges.

## Which one does your shoot need?

Ask what the background is *for*:

- **Should it disappear?** Clean white, grey or colour, nothing to add later → **cyclorama**.
- **Should it be replaced?** A location, a graphic, a virtual set → **chroma**.
- **Not sure what goes behind yet?** Chroma keeps the option open, at the cost of post-production
  time and a more careful shoot.
- **Is the budget tight on post?** Cyclorama. What you shoot is what you get.

## Questions to ask before you book

- How wide, deep and high is the cove — and how far back can the camera go?
- Is the cyc lit for your frame before you arrive, or is that part of your hours?
- What colour is it painted, and is there a charge for a repaint after your shoot?
- How large is the chroma backdrop, and can it be lit evenly across your whole frame?
- Is lighting and grip included in the rate, or itemised?

Send the studio your **frame size, the number of people in shot, and whether anything moves**. A
good studio will tell you honestly whether the floor covers it before you book.
