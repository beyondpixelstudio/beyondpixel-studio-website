A live stream is one-way. The room holds the event and the internet watches it.

A hybrid event is two-way, and that one difference changes almost everything about how it is
planned. There are now two audiences who both expect to take part — to ask questions, to hear
a speaker who may not be in the building, to feel that the event is happening *to them* rather
than near them. Get it right and a conference doubles its reach. Get it wrong and the online
half is watching a meeting they are not in.

## Live stream, hybrid event, webcast: the difference

- **Live stream.** The event is broadcast outward. Viewers watch; they may comment, but
  nothing they do reaches the room.
- **Hybrid event.** Remote participants are part of the programme: a speaker joining from
  Delhi, questions from online attendees read out on stage, a panel with two people in the
  room and one on a screen.
- **Webcast.** A stream to a *defined* audience — employees, shareholders, members, delegates —
  usually on a private or registered page rather than a public channel.

A single event can be all three: a hybrid conference, webcast privately to registered
delegates.

## The echo problem, and the fix called mix-minus

The first thing that goes wrong at most hybrid events is sound.

A remote speaker's voice comes into the venue and goes out through the PA. If the audio sent
back to that speaker includes the full room mix, they hear their own voice returning a second
later, delayed by the round trip across the internet. It is disorienting enough to make
experienced speakers stop mid-sentence.

The fix is a **mix-minus**: the remote speaker is sent everything in the room *except* their
own voice. They hear the moderator, the audience and the other panellists, but never
themselves. It is a routing decision made at the sound desk and the production switcher, and it
has to be set up and tested before the session — not discovered when the first remote
panellist says hello.

The same logic applies to microphones in the room. A laptop on the stage with its own
microphone open will feed the PA back into the call. Remote audio should come in and go out
through the desk, and nowhere else.

## Latency: the online audience is behind

A stream to YouTube or a similar platform typically reaches viewers several seconds — often
closer to half a minute — after it happens, depending on the platform's latency settings.

That matters the moment the event invites participation. A moderator who asks "any questions
online?" and waits five seconds will hear silence, because the online audience has not heard
the question yet. Two practical rules:

- **Collect online questions continuously** through the session, with one person reading them,
  rather than calling for them live.
- **Use a low-latency mode** where the platform offers one, for sessions that depend on
  back-and-forth.

Remote *speakers* on a video call are a different path with much lower delay, which is why they
can hold a conversation with the room while the public stream runs behind.

## What the room needs to see

Hybrid works when the people in the room can see the people who are not.

- Remote speakers on the **venue screens**, large enough to read expressions, switched in when
  they speak.
- A **confidence monitor** facing the stage, so presenters can see slides and remote panellists
  without turning their backs to the audience.
- The **same programme** on the venue screens and the stream wherever possible, so both
  audiences are watching one event rather than two.

## When a webcast is the better choice

Not every event should be public. Investor briefings, internal town halls, board-level
announcements, medical case discussions and member-only sessions all need an audience limited to
the people invited.

The options, from least to most controlled:

1. **Unlisted link.** Not searchable, but anyone who has the link can watch and forward it.
   Unlisted is *not* private.
2. **Password-protected player.** Access only with the password; better, though passwords get
   shared.
3. **Registration-gated page.** Viewers sign in individually, which also tells you exactly who
   attended — useful for compliance, CME credit or sales follow-up.

Choose by the question "what happens if the wrong person watches this?" If the answer is
"nothing much", unlisted is fine. If the answer is a problem, gate it.

## A planning checklist

- Which sessions have remote speakers, and do they have a wired connection and a headset?
- Is a mix-minus set up and tested with each remote speaker before doors open?
- Who collects online questions, and how do they reach the moderator?
- What do the venue screens show, and is there a confidence monitor?
- Public stream, unlisted link, password, or registration?
- Is the session recorded locally, independent of the stream and the call?
