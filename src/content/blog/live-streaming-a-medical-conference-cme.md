A medical conference is the most demanding stream most organisers will ever commission, and it
rarely looks like it on paper. There is no stage show, no performance, often no music. Just a
lectern, a slide deck, and an hour of material dense enough that a delegate who cannot read the
axis on a survival curve has effectively not attended.

That is the job: make dense clinical material legible, protect what should not be broadcast,
and — where credit hours are involved — prove who was there.

## Take the slides as a direct feed, never off the projector

The single biggest difference between a usable CME stream and a useless one is how the slides
are captured.

Filmed off the projector screen, a slide loses contrast, picks up the room's lighting, and turns
small type into grey smudge. A table of trial results becomes decoration. Taken as a **direct
feed from the presenter's laptop** into the switcher, the same slide arrives at full resolution,
exactly as the presenter designed it.

That means a capture point at the lectern, a tested cable run, and a plan for the moment a
speaker arrives with their own laptop five minutes before their slot. Build a short changeover
into the running order for it.

## The layouts a conference actually uses

A good operator moves between three looks through a session:

- **Slides full frame, speaker in a corner box.** The default while a slide is being explained.
  The delegate reads the slide and still sees who is talking.
- **Speaker full frame.** When the presenter steps away from the deck to make a point or tell a
  case story.
- **Panel wide.** For discussions, cutting to whoever is speaking.

Name straps with each speaker's name, designation and institution belong on screen at the start
of every talk. Prepare them from the programme in advance; typing them live is how
designations end up misspelt in front of five hundred colleagues.

## Patient images: streaming is publishing

Clinical presentations often contain patient photographs, scans with names in the corner, or
case details that identify someone. In a closed hall, that is a professional discussion. The
moment it is streamed, it is a publication — and a recording of it can outlive the event
indefinitely.

Settle this before the day, not during the session:

- **Ask presenters to confirm** their slides are de-identified, or that consent covers
  publication, as part of the speaker brief.
- **Stream clinical case sessions privately** — to registered delegates — rather than on a
  public channel.
- **Review the recording before releasing it publicly.** Any slide that should not be public
  can be cut from the edited version even if it went out to delegates live.
- **Give the operator a "slides off" instruction.** A presenter who realises mid-talk that a
  slide should not be broadcast can signal, and the switcher cuts to the speaker.

## Credit hours and proof of attendance

Continuing medical education credit in India is accredited by state medical councils, and
councils differ on how they treat online and hybrid attendance. Before the event, check with the
accrediting council:

- whether online attendance earns the same credit as physical attendance;
- what proof of attendance it needs — registration records, login logs, a post-session
  assessment; and
- whether that proof has to be submitted per session or per day.

Whatever the answer, it changes the platform. A public YouTube stream cannot tell you who
watched. A **registration-gated webcast** can, and it is far easier to design that in from the
start than to reconstruct attendance afterwards.

## Parallel halls

Large conferences run sessions side by side. Each hall that streams is its own production — its
own cameras, switcher, operator and slide feed — and each needs its own destination so a
delegate can choose. Budget and crew scale with the number of halls, not the number of days.

## A planning checklist

- Slide capture at every lectern, tested with a spare laptop.
- Name straps prepared from the final programme.
- Speaker brief covering de-identification and consent.
- Public, private or registration-gated — decided per session.
- Accrediting council's rules on online attendance confirmed in writing.
- One production per streaming hall.
- A local recording of every session, independent of the stream.
