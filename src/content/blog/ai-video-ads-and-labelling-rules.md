Our earlier piece on [where AI helps in video production](/blog/where-ai-helps-in-video-production/)
looked at the pipeline — the stages a model can carry and the ones it cannot. This one looks at
what brands actually commission AI video *for*, and at the part that changed in 2026: the labels.

## What brands use AI video for

### Ad variations for testing

Performance marketing lives on testing: five hooks, three backgrounds, two lengths, to see which
one converts. Shooting every variant is expensive. Generating variants from one approved concept
is not, and that is where AI video has become genuinely useful — the idea, the product and the
message are fixed and approved, and generation supplies the variations around them.

### Real products in generated scenes

A product photographed or filmed properly, then placed into a scene that would be costly or
impossible to shoot: a festival, a landscape, a kitchen in another city. The rule that keeps this
honest is simple — **the product stays real**. A generated version of what you sell is a picture of
something that does not exist, and an ad that shows it performing in a way the real one cannot is
a misleading claim, however it was made.

### Interior visualisation

An empty room taken to a finished interior — for interior designers selling a scheme, developers
selling a flat before it is furnished, hotels showing a renovation. It works because the buyer
understands they are seeing a proposal. It stays honest by being labelled as one: an artist's
impression, on screen, wherever it is used to sell. For a registered real estate project, it is
also an advertisement under RERA and carries the registration details like any other — see
[real estate video and RERA rules](/blog/real-estate-video-rera-rules-odisha/).

### Explainers and language versions

Software, services and processes that happen inside a machine are hard to film and easy to
illustrate. And a finished film can be versioned into several languages with synthetic or recorded
voiceover — useful for audiences across India.

## Where it still falls down

Generated video still struggles with the same things: hands and faces under scrutiny, legible text,
and consistency across a sequence longer than a few seconds. For an ad that depends on a real
person's performance or a product doing something specific, a camera is still the right tool.

## The labels: what is now required

### In India

Amendments to India's IT Rules, notified by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology
on **10 February 2026** and in force from **20 February 2026**, bring synthetically generated
content — AI-generated images, audio and video — into the due-diligence duties of online platforms.
Platforms must label synthetic content, and in practice they ask the people uploading it to declare
it. For a brand, that means the platform you advertise on will expect to know when an ad is
generated, and will label it.

### On YouTube

YouTube asks creators to disclose content that is **realistic and altered or synthetic** — in
particular when a video:

- makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not;
- alters footage of a real event or place; or
- generates a realistic-looking scene that did not actually happen.

Clearly unrealistic or animated content, and generative AI used only to assist production, do not
trigger the disclosure.

### On Facebook and Instagram

Meta labels AI-generated and AI-altered content as "AI info". Ads made with Meta's own generative
tools are labelled automatically, and realistic content judged deceptive can be labelled more
prominently and shown to fewer people.

### Advertising standards still apply

None of this replaces the older rule. The Advertising Standards Council of India's code expects
advertising claims to be truthful and capable of substantiation — and a generated demonstration of
what a product does is still a claim about the product.

## A simple rule of thumb

Ask one question of every generated shot: **could a reasonable viewer take this for a real
recording of a real person, place or event?** If yes, label it. If it is plainly stylised — motion
graphics, an obvious illustration — a label is usually not required, but disclosing costs nothing
and protects the brand if a viewer thinks otherwise.

And one line to hold regardless of the rules: **do not synthesise a real person** — a founder's
voice, an employee's face, a customer's testimonial — without that person's written consent.

> This is a practical summary for brands commissioning video, not legal advice. Platform policies
> change often; check the current rules for each platform before a campaign goes out.
