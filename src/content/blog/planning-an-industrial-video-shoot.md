Industrial films almost never fail for creative reasons. They fail at the gate: the crew
arrives without the right induction, a camera battery is not allowed into the area that was
the whole point of the shoot, the line the film was about is down for maintenance that day, or
the interview is recorded fifteen metres from a compressor and is unusable.

Every one of those is settled — or not — before the shoot day. This is the order to settle
them in.

## Start with the safety induction, not the shot list

Every plant has an EHS process, and the film crew is a contractor on site like any other. That
usually means an induction — sometimes a video, sometimes a briefing — before anyone goes past
reception, an escort for the whole visit, and restrictions on where visitors can walk.

Ask your EHS team three questions at the brief:

1. What induction does a visiting crew need, and how long does it take?
2. Which areas can the crew enter, and which need a separate permit?
3. Who is the escort, and are they available for the whole shoot window?

The answers reshape the schedule. A two-hour induction on a one-day shoot is a quarter of the
day gone before a camera is switched on.

## PPE on the crew — and correct PPE on camera

The crew needs whatever the site requires: hard hats, safety shoes, hi-vis, eye protection,
hearing protection near the line.

The less obvious point is the PPE of **the people being filmed**. An industrial film is watched
by the company's own safety function, by auditors and often by customers. A shot of an operator
without gloves at a machine that requires them is not a continuity error; it is a recorded
safety breach, published under the company's name. Check every setup against the area's PPE
rules before rolling, not in the edit.

## Hazardous zones are a hard limit

Refineries, gas-handling plants and some chemical units have **classified hazardous areas**
where flammable gas, vapour or dust may be present. In those zones an ordinary camera, light or
lithium battery is a potential ignition source.

Equipment used in those zones in India has to be certified for the zone — the Petroleum and
Explosives Safety Organisation (PESO) approves it — and ordinary equipment only goes in, if at
all, under the site's own work-permit system, usually with gas testing. Most corporate films of
such plants are therefore shot from outside the classified areas, or with long lenses from safe
positions. Find the zone boundaries on the site plan at the brief; they decide what the film can
show.

## Film the process running

The point of most plant films is the plant working. That means scheduling the shoot around
production, not the other way round:

- **When is the line running at a representative rate?** Not during a changeover, not during
  maintenance.
- **Where are the safe vantage points?** Walkways, gantries, observation windows.
- **Guards stay on.** Never ask for a guard to be removed for a cleaner shot. The shot is not
  worth it, and the film would be showing an unsafe act.

If the process only runs at night or in a short window, the shoot follows it.

## Sound: interviews away from the line

Plants are loud, and noise is the most common reason plant interviews get re-shot. Lapel
microphones pick up motors and compressors surprisingly well.

- **Record interviews in a quiet room** — an office, a control room, a training room — and use
  the line footage as picture over the voice.
- **Record the line's sound separately**, on purpose, so the edit has real ambience rather than
  library audio.

## Light: flicker at 50 Hz

Industrial lighting is a mix — high-bay LEDs, older discharge lamps, daylight through roof
panels — and under India's 50 Hz mains supply much of it flickers at a rate a camera can catch as
rolling bands. Shooting at a shutter speed of 1/50 or 1/100 of a second keeps the flicker out of
the picture. Test it on the day in each area; the bands do not show in a viewfinder glance.

## Confidentiality

Processes, layouts and some equipment can be commercially sensitive. Agree at the brief:

- which areas and screens must not appear;
- whether rushes need review by the site before the crew leaves; and
- who signs off the final cut for confidentiality, separately from the creative approval.

## Before the day: a checklist

- Induction booked, escort named, permitted areas marked on the site plan.
- Hazardous-zone boundaries identified.
- Production schedule confirmed for the shoot window.
- PPE list for crew and for people on camera.
- Quiet interview room reserved.
- Confidentiality rules and footage review agreed.
