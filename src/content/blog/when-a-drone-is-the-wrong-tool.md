A drone is in almost every brief we receive, and in a meaningful share of them it should not
be. Not because the shot is difficult or because the permissions are hard — often they are
neither — but because the shot does not do anything for the film it is going into.

This is written against our own interests, which is rather the point. Aerial work is a line
item we sell. It is worth more to us that it is in your budget when it earns its place and
out of it when it does not.

## What a drone is uniquely good at

There are four jobs an aerial camera does that nothing else does. Almost everything worth
flying for is one of these.

### 1. Scale that cannot be seen from the ground

A crowd of thirty thousand looks like a crowd of three hundred from head height. A campus,
a plant, a coastline, a procession — anything whose *size* is the story — is only legible
from above.

This is the strongest case for flying, and it is the one that survives every edit. If the
point of the shot is "look how big this is", ground cameras genuinely cannot do it.

### 2. Geography and relationship

Where the new building sits in relation to the old one. How the road reaches the site. Where
the river runs past the plant. A single aerial establishes in four seconds what a voice-over
needs two sentences and a map to explain.

### 3. Movement through a space that has no floor

A camera that rises from a face to reveal the whole campus behind them, or tracks alongside
a moving vehicle at first-floor height. These are moves a crane cannot reach and a handheld
cannot smooth.

### 4. The single establishing frame

One good aerial at the top of a film tells the viewer where they are. It is a legitimate,
useful, hard-working shot — and one is usually enough.

## When it is the wrong tool

### When the subject is a person

Faces are the reason to use a lens, not altitude. A drone shot of an interview subject is a
shot of the top of someone's head. If the film is about people — and most corporate films
are — the aerial is decoration around the part that matters.

### When the light is flat

Aerials live on texture: long shadows, side light, the shape of a building picked out by the
sun. Midday overhead light flattens all of it, and the same site that looks extraordinary at
seven in the morning looks like a satellite image at one in the afternoon.

If the only slot available is the middle of the day, the aerial will be the weakest shot in
the film. That is a scheduling problem, not a piloting one.

### When it is indoors, or effectively indoors

Enclosed halls, covered stages, anything with a ceiling and an audience underneath it. Small
indoor drones exist, and a flying camera above seated guests is a risk with very little
return. A slider or a gimbal does the same job on the ground, safely.

### When the site cannot be flown

Some locations cannot be flown at all, whatever the budget. Indian airspace is mapped by
coordinate, not by city — two sites four kilometres apart in Bhubaneswar can carry
completely different requirements, and a red-zone site is simply not available. We cover how
that map works in [drone permissions in Odisha](/blog/drone-permissions-odisha-dgca-rules/).

The relevant point for a budget is this: **the airspace check happens before the quote, not
after the booking.** If the aerial cannot be flown, you want that on day one, while there is
still time to plan a film that does not need it.

### When it is there because it is expected

The commonest reason a drone is in a brief is that drones are in briefs. A film with three
unmotivated aerials in it does not look more expensive; it looks like a showreel with a
company's name on the end.

## The ratio that works

For a two-to-three minute corporate film, **one to three aerial shots**, totalling perhaps
ten seconds of finished runtime. That is enough to establish the place and show the scale.
Past that, each additional aerial is taking screen time from the thing the film is actually
about.

For an event, usually one: the room or the ground, full, from above, early.

## What flying actually costs you on the day

Beyond the fee, an aerial has three costs people do not budget:

- **Time.** Setup, checks and a battery cycle is a real block of the shooting day, and it
  happens while the rest of the crew waits.
- **Weather.** Wind and rain stop flying, and the window that works for light is often the
  window that works for wind. A shot list that depends on an aerial has a weather dependency
  in it.
- **Permission lead time.** Where a site needs clearance, that is calendar time, not
  production time.

None of these are reasons not to fly. They are reasons to know on the day you plan the
shoot, rather than on the morning of it.

## The four questions to ask before it goes in the budget

1. **What does this shot say that a ground camera cannot say?** If the answer is "it looks
   nice", it is decoration.
2. **How many seconds of the finished film will it occupy?** Compare that to its share of
   the budget.
3. **What time of day will it be flown, and is that time available?**
4. **Has the location been checked for airspace, before anything was promised?**

Four honest answers usually resolve it. Sometimes the aerial is the best shot in the film and
should be protected at the cost of something else. Sometimes it comes out, and the money buys
a second shoot day, which improves everything.
