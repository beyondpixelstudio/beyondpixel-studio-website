"Can you do a drone survey of the site?" is a question with two very different answers, and the
difference decides the flight plan, the software, the time on site and what lands on your desk
afterwards.

One kind of survey is **seeing**: video and stills that show a site, a structure or a process from
angles nobody can safely reach on foot. The other is **measuring**: hundreds of overlapping
photographs stitched into a map or 3D model accurate enough to calculate a stockpile's volume. Both
are called surveys. They are not interchangeable.

## Survey video: inspection and documentation

A survey video is flown by a pilot choosing angles — orbits around a structure, slow passes along
a conveyor, a rise over a pit edge, a close look at a roof or a chimney from a safe distance.

It is the right tool for:

- **visual inspection** of assets too high, too large or too hazardous to walk — stacks, roofs,
  conveyors, pipelines, transmission lines;
- **documentation** for stakeholders, investors and management reviews;
- **progress reporting** that people can understand without a GIS tool;
- **incident and condition records** — what a structure looked like on a given date.

What it cannot do is **measure**. A beautiful orbit of a stockpile tells you it is large. It does
not tell you it holds how many tonnes.

## Mapping: photogrammetry and measured outputs

A mapping flight is flown by software, not by eye. The drone runs a grid of parallel passes over
the site, taking photographs at fixed intervals with heavy overlap — commonly 70–80% forward and
60–70% to the side — so that every point on the ground appears in many images. Processing software
then reconstructs the site as:

- an **orthomosaic** — a single, geometrically corrected aerial image you can measure distances
  and areas on;
- a **3D model or point cloud** of the terrain and structures;
- **volumes** of stockpiles, cut and fill;
- **contours and elevation models** for planning and drainage.

Accuracy depends on two things. **Ground sampling distance** — how much ground each pixel covers —
is set by flying height and camera. **Positional accuracy** comes from ground control points
surveyed on site, or from RTK/PPK positioning on the drone; without either, a map can look perfect
and still sit metres away from where it should be.

## Which one do you need?

| If you need to… | You want |
| --- | --- |
| Show the board how the site looks | Survey video |
| Inspect a stack, roof or conveyor | Survey video, close and slow |
| Calculate stockpile volumes | Mapping |
| Measure areas or distances on site | Mapping (orthomosaic) |
| Track construction for buyers or investors | Survey video on a repeatable path |
| Compare progress against the drawings | Mapping, flown periodically |

Many industrial clients need both — a monthly measured map for the engineers and a two-minute
progress film for everyone else — and both can come from the same site visit if planned together.

## Construction progress: the repeatable path

For progress, the value is in comparison. Month three is only useful if it lines up against month
one. That means the **same flight path, altitude, heading and time of day** every cycle, saved and
re-flown rather than improvised. Done well, a developer can cut every month together into a single
time-lapse of the building rising — one of the most persuasive assets a project can publish.

## The rules still apply

Industrial sites are not exempt from drone regulation. Airspace is checked against the DGCA map for
the exact coordinates — see our guide to [drone permissions in Odisha](/blog/drone-permissions-odisha-dgca-rules/) —
and written permission comes from whoever controls the site. Mines, ports and plants also run their
own safety regimes: flights are planned around blasting schedules, crane movements, vehicle routes
and exclusion zones, with a ground brief with the site and security teams before take-off.

## What to ask before you commission a survey

- **What is the deliverable?** Video, stills, an orthomosaic, a 3D model, volumes?
- **How accurate does it need to be?** Centimetres for engineering, or "clearly visible" for a
  report?
- **How often?** One-off, monthly, or at each construction milestone?
- **What format?** A video file, or GIS formats your engineers can open?
- **Who owns the data**, and how long is it kept?
- **What permissions and site inductions are needed**, and who arranges them?
